# Last Call

*A short story. ~1,800 words.*

---

The town is called Briar Hollow, and it has six telephone lines.

Well — seven, if you count the trucker payphone out on Highway 40 at marker 88, but that one mostly lights up in summer when the long-haul drivers want to call home. And eight, if you count County General over in Cherokee, which the board hardly ever rings through to because by the time anyone needs County General, the news is already on its way some other way.

For twenty-six years the operator has sat at the wooden panel on the second floor of the post office. She knows the voices. She knows whose voice is rough on a Monday morning. She knows whose voice goes thin when bad news is coming. She knows whose voice forgets to say goodbye. She knows who never calls.

Tomorrow at six in the morning, the new automated exchange goes online, and the lines will route themselves.

Tonight is her last shift.

---

She comes in at eight. The desk lamp is the same lamp it has always been — a brass arm bent like a question mark over the board, the bulb glowing a soft honey that picks out the brass tips of the cables and the labels on the sockets. **PATTY'S. COLE'S. CHAPEL. SHERIFF. WHEELER. HAYES.** There is a coffee cup that says EXCHANGE OPERATOR on one side and a chipped circle of mother-of-pearl glaze on the other. There is a small mint-coloured indicator at the top of the board with three filaments inside it, and each filament is a chance.

She pours coffee. She sits.

The first call comes in at nine-fifteen.

---

It's Patty.

*"Mornin', operator,"* Patty says, which is what Patty calls everyone at all hours of the day or night because Patty's day starts at four-thirty and ends sometime around when other people's start. *"Patty here. My delivery truck won't crank — could you put me through to Cole's Garage?"*

The operator plugs in. The cable's brass tip slides into Cole's brass socket and the metal of the ring fits perfectly, the way it has for twenty-six years. There is a tiny *thunk* and then a tone and then Cole picks up on the first ring.

*"Cole's. Yep, I'll be over in twenty."*

Click.

This was the verb of every night for twenty-six years. Click.

---

The second call comes from a phone booth.

She hears the wind before the voice. Diesel passing in waves behind a glass that is colder than it should be on a November night. A young man clears his throat.

*"Hello? Operator? I need to reach Mrs. Hayes on 7th and Vine. She's my mother — she didn't answer her morning call. I'm two hours out on Highway 40."*

Daniel Hayes lives in Greensboro now. He calls his mother every Sunday morning at ten and every Wednesday evening at seven, and he has done so since the year he turned eighteen, and the operator has plugged each of those calls in herself.

She plugs in **HAYES**.

The line rings. Rings. Rings.

Margaret Hayes does not pick up.

Daniel breathes through the static. *"She… she must've gone to the market. I'll try again later. Thanks."*

The operator pulls the cable. The brass tip is a little warm.

There is no market in Briar Hollow that opens before noon, and Margaret Hayes never went to the market anyway. Margaret Hayes had her groceries delivered by Cole's boy, on Tuesdays.

The operator lights a cigarette she will not finish.

---

At ten-oh-five Mrs. Henley calls from her kitchen window. Mrs. Henley calls from her kitchen window roughly once a week, usually about someone parking on the wrong side of the street or about the noise from the chapel bells on Sunday, and the operator has never quite known whether to be grateful or annoyed that Mrs. Henley exists.

Tonight Mrs. Henley says, *"Operator, this is Henley over on 8th. There's been an ambulance sitting two streets over near Vine for ten minutes. Connect me to the sheriff, would you? Something's not right."*

The operator plugs in **SHERIFF**.

Briggs picks up on the second ring. He says, *"Briggs,"* the way he always does — once, sharp, no surname — and then Mrs. Henley tells him about the ambulance, and Briggs says, *"Appreciate the call, Mrs. Henley. We're already on it. Stay inside."*

He is calm. He is too calm.

The operator pulls the cable and does not light a second cigarette.

---

The Reverend calls in at ten-forty about old Mr. Bray, who has had another spell during evening prayer. The Reverend calls about Mr. Bray roughly once a season. The operator plugs in **WHEELER** and Doc Wheeler says, *"On my way, Reverend,"* and that is that.

It is the only call tonight that ends the way calls used to end.

---

At eleven-twelve, Daniel calls again.

He is no longer in a phone booth. He has pulled over at a gas station somewhere outside Asheville and the engine is still running. She can hear a radio playing somewhere behind him — a faint country song, half-buried in the static of the line.

*"Operator, it's Daniel Hayes again. Still no answer at Mom's. Could you try Doc Wheeler? She had a check-up Thursday — maybe he knows if she went somewhere."*

She plugs in **WHEELER**.

Doc picks up. There is a pause that is the longest pause the operator has heard on her board in twenty-six years. Then Doc Wheeler — who has not raised his voice on a phone line since the autumn of 1944 — says, *"Daniel… your mother came in Thursday with chest pains. I told her to call me at the first sign it came back. Son — drive faster."*

She pulls the cable. She doesn't drink the coffee. The coffee has gone cold.

---

At eleven-thirty Sheriff Briggs calls. He is calling the diner.

*"Patty? Briggs. Has Margaret Hayes been in today? Henley says there's a unit at her place."*

Patty's voice is small. *"She came in yesterday morning. Said she was tired. Hasn't been in today, Sheriff. Oh god."*

The operator pulls the cable carefully, the way you pull a covering back from something you do not want to look at.

---

At eleven-fifty-eight Daniel calls a third time. He does not say hello. He says, *"Operator — the Chapel, Reverend Carter. Hurry, please."*

She plugs in **CHAPEL**. Reverend Carter, who has been waiting by the phone for forty minutes, picks up on the half-ring.

*"I'm leaving now, son. I'll be at your mother's door in five. Stay on the road."*

Daniel doesn't say thank you. He doesn't say anything.

She holds the cable in her hand for a long second after the line goes dead, the way you hold a hand whose pulse you are checking.

---

Cole's truck breaks down at twelve-fifteen near 7th and Vine. He calls the diner from a corner phone because Patty is the only person in town who is awake at this hour, and his words come out faster than they normally do — *"Patty, it's Cole. Truck stalled out near 7th and Vine — there's two units and a chaplain at Margaret's place. Anyone heard from Daniel?"*

*"He's on Highway 40. Lord, he doesn't know yet."*

The chaplain at Margaret Hayes' door is Reverend Carter. The Reverend was supposed to be the one to tell Daniel.

There is a long silence on the diner's end of the line and then Patty says, very softly, *"Oh."*

The operator pulls the cable. Three of the six lit sockets have gone dark.

The mint-coloured indicator at the top of her board still has two filaments glowing.

---

At twelve-thirty-one the Reverend calls back.

His voice is steady but it is the steadiness of a man holding a steering wheel in a storm.

*"Operator. I need to reach Daniel Hayes — he's on the highway. Try the truck stop payphone at marker 88. Tell him his mother's alive. Tell him they're taking her to County General."*

There is no socket labelled **MARKER 88** on the operator's board.

She knows this because she has stared at the board for twenty-six years.

But the operator looks down, and there it is — a small brass socket on the lower right of the panel that has been dark every other night of her career and is, tonight, glowing the soft amber of the lamp. The label below it is hand-lettered in fountain-pen ink, by a hand that is not her own and may not be anyone's.

It says: **Highway 40 / MM 88**.

She plugs in.

The cable's brass tip slides into the brass socket and there is a tiny *thunk*. Static. Static that lasts long enough she starts to fear it will not break. Then a voice — a stranger, a trucker, breath ragged — says, *"Yeah? Hello?"*

The Reverend tells the trucker.

The trucker drops the phone onto the side of the booth — the operator hears it bang against the glass — and there is a long silence and then somewhere very far away, very faint, almost lost in the diesel of an idling rig, the voice of Daniel Hayes.

*"Tell the Reverend — tell him I'm coming."*

The operator pulls the cable. Her hand is shaking, which is something her hand has not done since the autumn of 1944.

---

At twelve-fifty-three the board goes quiet.

This is the longest the board has been quiet on a Saturday night in three years. The operator watches the six lit sockets — and the seventh that should not exist, still glowing — and the mint indicator with its two filaments, and she does not move.

At one-twenty she eats half a sandwich.

At two-forty-four she rinses out the coffee cup that says EXCHANGE OPERATOR. There is no more coffee.

At three-eleven the eighth socket lights up. She did not see it light up. She only sees that it is lit. The label, again, is in a hand that is not her own. **COUNTY GENERAL.**

The phone rings.

She picks up the receiver herself, which she has never done in twenty-six years.

It is Daniel.

*"Operator… please. County General. I'm in the parking lot. I need to know if I can still — "*

She plugs in.

She doesn't breathe until the line connects, and then she breathes only because she has to.

A nurse picks up — a voice she does not know, a voice that does not belong to Briar Hollow — and the nurse says, *"Mr. Hayes? She's awake. She's been asking for you."*

The line goes warm with quiet.

The operator does not pull the cable.

She lets the cable stay plugged in for as long as Daniel is on the line, which is a long time, because Daniel is crying quietly and the nurse on the other end is being kind. And after a while, when the line clicks off, the operator pulls the cable very slowly and lays it across her knee.

She is going to remember the weight of it.

---

The board goes dark, one light at a time. **HAYES** first. Then **CHAPEL**. Then **WHEELER**. Then **SHERIFF**. Then **PATTY'S**. Then **COLE'S**. The seventh and eighth sockets — **MM 88** and **COUNTY GENERAL** — flicker once and then fade, as though they had never been there, which perhaps is the truth.

At three-forty-seven the operator stands. She switches off the lamp. The mint-coloured indicator at the top of the board still has one filament burning, and she leaves it burning.

She walks down the stairs and out of the post office. The town smells of cold wet leaves and somebody's woodstove. Far off, on the highway, a truck is heading east.

At six o'clock the new exchange goes online.

The lines route themselves.

---

*Shift ended, 03:47.*
*Margaret Hayes was discharged six days later.*
*The new automated exchange went live at sunrise.*
